Balancing (TBMM)

The TBMM (Electric Avenue) supports automatic active balancing of modules outside the pack. Good candidate for Module 16 (confirmed 146 mV real imbalance) once sense wires are repaired.

How to balance with TBMM (from electric-avenue.us/tbmm and demo):
  • Connect TBMM to the module’s BMB connector with the included cable.
  • Power via USB-C (wall adapter, power bank, or phone).
  • Diagnose voltages first and confirm board health.
  • Use the Wi-Fi web interface to set the balance point (target voltage) and turn balancing on/off — easiest method.
  • Let it run; demo showed reduction from ~10–18 mV to ~2 mV over hours to ~24 h.
  • Monitor progress on the display or web interface. Retest imbalance after.

How to choose the balance point

The balance point is the target voltage the TBMM balances the cell groups toward.

For Module 16 (current range ~3.681 V low to 3.827 V high):

  • 3.750 V — roughly the middle of the current spread
  • 3.720–3.740 V — slightly more conservative (pulls the high groups down more)

General approach:

  • Look at the live cell-group voltages on the TBMM.
  • Pick a voltage below the highest groups and near or above the lowest ones.
  • Stay comfortably inside the normal range for these modules (roughly 3.6–4.0 V).
  • Set it in the web interface, turn balancing on, and monitor. You can adjust the point later if progress stalls.

After balancing, recheck the imbalance. Goal is ≤ 6 mV to match the pass criteria.

What “Connector Issue” Means

On Module 12 the site notes a connector issue because the large voltage spread reported by Scan My Tesla disappeared when the module was measured directly with the TBMM.

  • SMT (June 2026): Module 12 showed an 81 mV spread.
  • TBMM (direct test): Same module measured only 4 mV imbalance and PASSed. Cell groups were tightly matched.

The cells themselves were healthy. The BMS was simply reading them incorrectly through the connector / sense-lead path. This is a false or inflated reading, not real cell imbalance.

Why This Happens

The BMS reads cell-group voltages through sense leads and BMB (Battery Management Board) connectors. Anything that adds resistance or corrosion in that path can make the BMS report a fake spread even when the cells are fine.

Common causes on these packs:

  • Water intrusion (confirmed in the pyro-fuse cavity on this pack)
  • Corrosion on connector pins or sense wires
  • Loose or high-resistance contacts
  • Damaged or oxidized sense leads
  • BMB board issues that affect voltage sensing (related to BMS_a129)

Module 14 showed the same pattern (low true imbalance on TBMM). Module 16 is the opposite: its 146 mV TBMM result confirmed a real cell-group imbalance caused by detached sense wires.

SMT vs TBMM

  • SMT reads through the vehicle’s BMS and the full connector chain. It can show inflated spreads if connectors or sense wiring are bad.
  • TBMM connects more directly to the module and measures the actual cell groups. A big drop from SMT spread to TBMM spread is strong evidence of a connector/sense problem rather than cell degradation.

Healthy modules typically show ≤ 6 mV imbalance on TBMM. Anything significantly higher is treated as a real issue until proven otherwise.

Next Diagnostic Steps

For modules that show a connector-style pattern (high SMT, low TBMM):

  • Inspect and reseat the BMB connector
  • Check sensor leads for corrosion, damage, or high resistance
  • Visually inspect the BMS circuit board for corrosion or damage
  • Clean contacts and retest

Sense Wire Repair Guide (Module 16)

Module 16 has two detached sensor (voltage sense) wires causing measurement issues. The collector plates are aluminum, so the original ultrasonic welds can fail from corrosion or mechanical stress (common with water intrusion).

Riveting vs Soldering / Welding

Riveting (recommended for DIY):

  • Advantages: Strong mechanical connection, no heat near cells, low resistance when clean, uses basic tools, proven in the field for these modules.
  • Disadvantages: Requires a small drilled hole (control shavings), careful wire routing to avoid pinching, potential galvanic corrosion (mitigate with antioxidant compound).

Soldering / Welding:

  • Soldering to aluminum is unreliable with normal tools (oxide layer + high thermal mass). Risk of heat damage to cells or plastic. Connection often not durable.
  • Ultrasonic or laser welding matches the factory method but requires specialized equipment not available for basic DIY.

How to Rivet a Sense Wire (Basic DIY)

Tools & materials: Hand pop-rivet tool, small aluminum pop rivets (3/32" typical), small ring terminals, thin copper wire (similar gauge), strippers/crimper, drill + bit, isopropyl alcohol, fine sandpaper, antioxidant compound (Noalox or Penetrox A), Kapton tape.

  1. Confirm the bad sense wires (tug-test and voltage/resistance check). On Module 16 these are the detached orange and green wires on the bottom.
  2. Free or cut the bad wire near the failed attachment. Splice an extension if needed (butt connector + heat shrink).
  3. Crimp a small ring terminal onto the wire end. Pull-test the crimp.
  4. Choose a solid edge or overhang on the collector plate for that brick. Clean thoroughly with isopropyl and lightly scuff the aluminum contact area.
  5. Drill a hole sized for the rivet. Capture all metal shavings.
  6. Apply a thin layer of antioxidant compound to the ring terminal face and cleaned aluminum.
  7. Set the aluminum pop rivet so the terminal sits flat and tight.
  8. Route the wire neatly with a gentle service loop. Secure with Kapton tape so it cannot vibrate or get pinched.
  9. Verify with multimeter or TBMM: low resistance and consistent brick voltages.

After repair, retest Module 16 with TBMM and consider balancing if the true imbalance remains. Document photos and results in the Physical Inspection and Balancing tables.

Safety: Modules hold significant energy even when removed. Isolate the module under repair, wear eye protection when drilling, and keep metal debris out of the cells.
